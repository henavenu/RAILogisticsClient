Why a model card exists
Orientation

The model is not a detail — it's a dependency

In one breath An agent is only as trustworthy, available, and compliant as the model underneath it. A model card is the label on that engine — so you can govern the model, not just the agent.

Two agents can look identical in the registry and behave completely differently because of the model wired underneath. One might run on a frontier API in a foreign jurisdiction that could be cut off by a sanction; the other on weights you host in your own data center. Same agent card, radically different risk.

Sovereign AI and model-independent control are the reasons this section exists. Sovereign AI means you can answer "whose infrastructure, whose law, whose kill-switch?" for every model in production. Model-independent control means your governance — policies, audit, kill-switches, human-in-the-loop — sits above any single model, so no one vendor's outage, price change, or policy shift can hold your agents hostage.

The model card turns that from a slogan into a checklist. Each field below answers one governance question: who holds the engine, where does it run, what can it cost me, what am I promised about my data, and can I prove it in an audit?

01
Classification
Classification · 1.1

Model type

In one breath What kind of AI engine this is and how big it is. "70B dense transformer" means 70 billion tunable dials, all used on every word it reads or writes.

The model type tells you the architecture and the size, which together set the ceiling on capability, speed, and cost.

  • Transformer — the near-universal architecture behind modern LLMs. It reads a sequence of tokens and predicts the next one, using an "attention" mechanism to weigh how much each earlier token matters. Everything on your cards is a transformer.
  • Dense vs Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) — in a dense model (like Llama 3.3 70B) every parameter is active for every token. In an MoE model a router activates only a few "expert" sub-networks per token, so a model can hold huge total parameters but only pay compute for a slice — cheaper to run at a given quality.
  • Parameters (the "70B", "8B") — the count of learned numbers. More parameters generally means more capability and more compute per token. It's a rough proxy for power, not a guarantee.
  • Reasoning / "thinking" models — models that generate a hidden chain of intermediate tokens before answering (e.g. Claude Opus 5's adaptive thinking). They trade latency and output tokens for accuracy on hard, multi-step problems.

For governance the type matters because it drives the failure surface: a small dense model is cheap and predictable but weaker at nuance; a frontier reasoning model is powerful but slower, pricier, and more prone to over-elaboration.

On your model cardsPharma­Logistics and MyBank both run dense Llama models (70B and 8B). Claude Opus 5 is shown as a frontier reasoning model — the fallback tier in your .env priority list.
Classification · 1.2

Weights

In one breath The weights are the trained model — billions of numbers learned from data. Who holds those numbers decides who really controls the AI.

During training, a model is shown enormous amounts of text and its internal numbers are nudged, again and again, until it predicts language well. Those final numbers — the weights (also called parameters or the checkpoint) — are the entire learned model. A weights file plus the architecture code is a runnable model; without the weights you have an empty shell.

This is the single most important governance fact about any model, because whoever holds the weights holds the engine:

  • Open-weight models (Llama, Mistral, Gemma) publish the weights for download. You can copy them, run them on your own hardware, inspect them, fine-tune them, and keep running them even if the original publisher disappears.
  • Closed / proprietary models (Claude, Gemini, GPT) never release the weights. You reach them only through the provider's API. The capability is rented, not owned.

"Open-weight" is not the same as "open-source": you usually get the weights but not the training data or the training code, and often under a license with conditions (see 1.6). Weights also can't be meaningfully read by a human — they're opaque numbers — so "open" means runnable and re-hostable, not transparent.

Why it's load-bearing for sovereignty: if you hold the weights, no vendor can unilaterally switch your agents off. If you don't, your continuity depends entirely on the provider's terms, pricing, and goodwill — which is exactly what withdrawal risk (1.5) measures.

Classification · 1.3

Provider (vs developer)

In one breath Two different companies can matter: the one that built the model and the one that runs it for you. They're often not the same.

The card separates two roles that people casually merge:

  • Developer / model maker — who trained the weights and is responsible for the model's capabilities, safety training, and license. Example: Meta develops Llama.
  • Provider / host — whose infrastructure actually serves the inference request you send. Example: Groq serves Llama on its custom LPU hardware; Vertex AI serves Gemini and can also serve Claude.

The distinction is not pedantic — your contract, your data-handling promises, your certifications, your uptime SLA, and your bill usually come from the provider, while the model's behavior and license come from the developer. The same Llama 3.3 70B served by Groq, by AWS Bedrock, or by your own servers gives you three different sets of guarantees, regions, and prices for one identical model.

When you assess a model, read both: a trustworthy developer served by an untrustworthy host is still an untrustworthy deployment, and vice versa.

Classification · 1.4

Substrate & custody

In one breath "Substrate" is where the model physically lives and who operates it. It's the difference between renting an engine and owning one.

Substrate combines two questions — are the weights open or closed? and who runs the inference? — into one custody model. There are four common ones, from least to most control:

SubstrateWeights held byInference run by
Proprietary hostedProviderProvider
Open-weight, externally hostedOpen (re-hostable)A third party
Open-weight, self-hostedYouYou
Sovereign / nationalA national jurisdictionThat jurisdiction

Custody is the plain-language version of the same idea: who could, in principle, take the model away from you or see the data flowing through it. Proprietary hosted means the provider holds both the engine and your traffic; self-hosted means you hold both; sovereign means a specific state's law and procurement govern both.

This field is the backbone of the sovereign-AI story, because it directly determines withdrawal risk (next) and shapes every data-control promise below it. A model card that only told you "it's Llama" would hide the fact that Llama-via-Groq and Llama-on-your-own-GPUs are worlds apart in custody.

On your model cardsLlama-via-Groq shows as open-weight, externally hosted; Claude Opus 5 as proprietary hosted; the sovereign EU option as open-weight, self-hosted / on-prem. Same governance layer above all three — that's model-independent control in action.
Classification · 1.5

Withdrawal risk

In one breath The chance that access to this model gets pulled out from under you — by a price hike, a shutdown, or a sanction. Higher risk means less control over your own continuity.

Withdrawal risk asks a continuity question: if this model became unavailable tomorrow, how exposed am I? It follows directly from substrate, and the card grades it in three tiers:

High Medium Low
  • High  Proprietary hosted. Access can be revoked unilaterally — model deprecation, a price or terms change, capacity throttling, export controls, or sanctions. You have no fallback except migrating to another model.
  • Medium  Open-weight but externally hosted, or region-pinnable proprietary. The specific endpoint can still be withdrawn or repriced, but you have an escape hatch: re-host the open weights elsewhere, or pin to another region/vendor.
  • Low  Self-hosted open weights. No one can unilaterally switch it off. The trade-off is that you now own patching, safety tuning, evaluation, and uptime.

Withdrawal risk is not the same as "quality" or "safety" — a high-risk frontier model may be the best performer available. It's a dependency risk, and it's exactly what the emergency Kill & Replace control on the model card is designed to mitigate: if a high-risk model is compromised or cut off, you re-bind every agent on it to a lower-risk alternative in one move.

Classification · 1.6

License

In one breath The legal terms for using the model. It decides whether you can self-host it, fine-tune it, and use its output commercially — or whether you're limited to the vendor's API rules.

The license is the contract that governs the weights or the API. It's a governance field, not a footnote, because it constrains your options for mitigating every other risk on the card.

  • Permissive open licenses (e.g. Apache 2.0, MIT) — self-host, modify, redistribute, and use commercially with minimal conditions. Maximum flexibility.
  • Community / source-available licenses (e.g. the Llama Community License) — open weights you can self-host and fine-tune, but with conditions: an acceptable-use policy, an attribution requirement, and thresholds (very large deployments need a separate agreement). Open enough to give you sovereignty, but read the fine print.
  • Proprietary commercial API terms (Claude, Gemini, GPT) — you don't get the weights at all; you get a right to call the API under the provider's usage policy, data terms, and rate limits. This is where the data-control promises in Section 4 come from — they are contractual, defined by these terms.

Two things reviewers check most: whether the output can be used commercially and to train competing models, and whether self-hosting or fine-tuning is permitted. The answer determines whether "just re-host it" is actually a legal option when withdrawal risk bites.

02
Hosting & residency
Hosting & residency · 2.1

Hosting region

In one breath The physical place — the data center and country — where your prompts are actually processed. Geography decides which laws apply to your data.

When your agent calls a model, the request travels to a specific data center somewhere on earth and is processed there. The hosting region names that place. It matters for three concrete reasons:

  • Jurisdiction. Data processed in a country is subject to that country's laws — including lawful-access and disclosure powers. An EU citizen's data processed in the US sits under different legal exposure than the same data processed in Frankfurt.
  • Latency. Physics: a region far from your users adds round-trip milliseconds to every call.
  • Availability & blast radius. A single region is a single point of failure; a regional outage or a regional policy change affects only agents pinned there.

Proprietary providers increasingly let you pin the region (Vertex AI regions like europe-west; Anthropic's inference_geo). Shared open-weight endpoints often don't — you get "provider default," usually US. "Global / multi-region" means the provider may route anywhere for capacity, which is great for uptime and weak for residency guarantees.

Hosting & residency · 2.2

Data residency

In one breath A promise about where your data stays — not just where it's processed, but where it's stored and that it won't quietly leave that boundary.

Hosting region (2.1) is where the compute runs; data residency is the stronger, contractual promise that your data — the request, any logs, any cached content — stays within a defined geographic or legal boundary and is not copied out of it.

The distinction matters because a request can be processed in the EU while logs, backups, or support access still flow to the US. A residency guarantee closes that gap. Reviewers grade it on a spectrum:

  • Full residency — data never leaves the boundary (the norm for self-hosted / sovereign; on your card, the EU on-prem option).
  • Region-pinned with commitments — the provider contractually keeps processing and storage in-region (available on enterprise Vertex, configurable on Anthropic via inference_geo).
  • No guarantee — shared endpoints (e.g. the public Groq endpoint) make no residency commitment; treat data as leaving your boundary.

Residency is the field that regulators (GDPR, sector rules) and customers ask about first. It pairs tightly with no-train and zero data retention in Section 4: residency says where, those say whether it's kept and reused at all.

03
Capacity & cost
Capacity & cost · 3.1

Tokens

In one breath Models don't read words — they read tokens, little chunks of text. Roughly, one token is about four characters, and ~750 words is about 1,000 tokens.

A token is the unit a model actually processes. Text is broken into tokens by a tokenizer — common words become one token, rarer words split into pieces ("tokenization" → token + ization), and spaces and punctuation count too. Numbers, code, and non-English text tend to use more tokens per character.

Everything else in this section is denominated in tokens, so it's the base unit you need:

  • Rough conversions (English): ~4 characters ≈ 1 token; ~¾ of a word ≈ 1 token; 1,000 tokens ≈ 750 words ≈ 1½ pages.
  • Input vs output tokens are counted and priced separately (see 3.4). A long document you send is input; the model's reply is output.
  • Tokenizers differ by model, so the same text can be a different token count on different models — which changes both cost and how much fits in the context window.

Practical upshot for operators: token counts, not word counts, drive both your bill and your capacity limits. A 50-page contract isn't "50 pages" to the model — it's roughly 25,000 tokens that must fit inside the context window and get paid for at the input rate.

Capacity & cost · 3.2

Context window

In one breath The model's working memory — the most it can "hold in mind" at once, counting everything you send plus everything it writes. Go over it and the oldest content falls off.

The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can attend to in a single request. It's a hard ceiling that covers the whole exchange: system prompt + conversation history + documents you attach + the model's own reply (and, for reasoning models, the hidden thinking tokens).

Why it matters:

  • It bounds how much the model can consider. A 128K window (Llama) holds roughly a short book; a 1M window (Claude Opus 5, Gemini 2.5) holds a large codebase or a stack of contracts at once.
  • Overflow is lossy. Exceed it and something must be dropped, summarized, or "compacted" — the model can silently lose earlier context, which is a correctness and audit concern.
  • Bigger isn't free. Filling a huge window costs more (you pay per input token) and can slow responses. Large windows are a capability, not a default to max out.

For long-running agents, the context window is the constraint behind features like history compaction and memory files — mechanisms to work around the ceiling without silently losing state.

Capacity & cost · 3.3

Max output tokens

In one breath The most the model can write in a single reply. Set it too low and long answers get cut off mid-sentence.

Max output is the cap on how many tokens the model may generate in one response. It's separate from — and always smaller than — the context window, and on reasoning models it must also cover the hidden thinking tokens.

  • Truncation risk. If a task needs a 40-page report but the output cap is set low, the reply stops abruptly (a "max_tokens" stop) and you must continue or retry. Reviewers check that the cap matches the job.
  • Latency and cost scale with it. Output tokens are generated one at a time and are the expensive half of the bill (3.4), so a generous cap is capacity you may pay for.
  • Provider ceilings vary. Frontier models allow very large outputs (Claude Opus 5 up to 128K tokens, typically via streaming); hosted open-weight endpoints often cap lower (Groq caps Llama outputs well below the context window).

Rule of thumb: the context window is how much it can read; max output is how much it can write back.

Capacity & cost · 3.4

Cost (input vs output)

In one breath You pay per million tokens, and writing costs more than reading — often about five times more. That's why long, chatty answers get expensive fast.

Model pricing is quoted per million tokens, split into two rates:

ModelInput / 1MOutput / 1M
Llama 3.3 70B (Groq)$0.59$0.79
Llama 3.1 8B (Groq)$0.05$0.08
Gemini 2.5 Flash$0.30$2.50
Claude Opus 5$5.00$25.00

Why output costs more: input tokens are processed in parallel in one pass, but output tokens are generated serially — the model runs a full forward pass for every single token it writes. That sequential work is the expensive part, so output rates run roughly 3–5× input rates.

  • Prompt caching can cut repeated input cost by ~90% — if the same big system prompt or document prefix is reused across calls, the provider bills the cached portion at a fraction of the normal rate.
  • Reasoning models bill their thinking. Hidden thinking tokens count as output, so a "cheap-looking" answer can carry a large invisible output cost. Effort/verbosity controls exist to manage this.
  • Self-hosted has no per-token price — you pay infrastructure (GPUs, power, ops) instead. That flips the economics at high volume and removes per-call metering entirely, which is part of the sovereign option's appeal.

For governance, cost is also a control surface: the model card's cost figures let you compare the true expense of a high-risk frontier model against a cheaper self-hosted one before an emergency forces the choice.

04
Controls
Controls · 4.1

No-train on inference

In one breath A promise that the provider won't use your prompts and the model's answers to train future models. What you send stays yours — it doesn't become part of the next model's brain.

"Inference" is just the act of using a trained model to get an answer (as opposed to "training," which is building the model). Every request your agent makes is an inference call. No-train on inference is the commitment that the content of those calls — your inputs and the model's outputs — will not be fed back into training a new or improved model.

This is the single most important data-governance promise on the card, and it's frequently misunderstood, so precisely:

  • What it prevents: your proprietary prompts, customer data, and the model's responses being absorbed into a future model's weights, where they could — in principle — surface in someone else's answer or be memorized verbatim.
  • What it does not by itself cover: whether the data is logged or stored at all. That's a separate promise — zero data retention (4.2). A provider can honor no-train while still keeping logs for abuse monitoring; conversely, no retention implies no training.
  • Where it comes from: it's contractual, set by the provider's data terms and your tier. Enterprise/API tiers (Anthropic, enterprise OpenAI, Groq, Vertex enterprise) are typically no-train by default; some consumer/free tiers train on data unless you opt out. Always verify by tier, not by brand.
  • Self-hosted answer: if you run the weights yourself, the question is moot — the data never leaves your infrastructure, so there's nothing to train on. This is why the sovereign option lists it as inherent rather than promised.

For a regulated buyer, "no-train" is the difference between "we use this vendor" and "legal signed off." It's what lets you route sensitive banking or pharma data through a third-party model without that data leaking into the vendor's product.

Controls · 4.2

Zero data retention (ZDR)

In one breath The provider keeps no copy of your request or the answer once it's been processed. Nothing is logged, so there's nothing to leak, subpoena, or breach later.

Where no-train (4.1) is about reuse, zero data retention is about storage. Under ZDR the provider processes your request, returns the answer, and retains none of it — no request logs, no response logs, no cached copies beyond the moment of processing.

  • Why it's stronger than no-train: retained logs are a standing liability even if never used for training — they can be breached, accessed by insiders, or compelled by legal process. ZDR removes the target entirely.
  • The trade-off: providers often keep short-term logs specifically for abuse and safety monitoring. Turning on ZDR can mean forgoing some of that safety tooling, and it may be gated to enterprise agreements or exclude certain features.
  • How it's offered: typically a contractual option you must enable (e.g. Anthropic's zero-data-retention agreements), not a universal default.

ZDR is what you reach for with the most sensitive workloads — PHI, PII, privileged material — where "we promise not to train on it" isn't enough and the answer has to be "there is no copy."

Controls · 4.3

Data-residency guarantee / region pinning

In one breath A control that forces the model to run in a chosen country and keep your data there. The lever behind the residency promise in 2.2.

This is the operational control that delivers the residency property from Section 2. Instead of accepting the provider's default routing, you pin the deployment to a specific region and get a contractual commitment that processing and storage stay there.

  • Vertex AI: choose a regional endpoint (europe-west4, us-central1, …) so inference physically runs in that jurisdiction.
  • Anthropic: the inference_geo parameter routes requests to a chosen region (e.g. US or EU).
  • Self-hosted / sovereign: residency is absolute — the model runs where your hardware is, full stop.

Reviewers treat this as the enforcement mechanism for data-sovereignty commitments to regulators and customers. Without pinning, "we process in the EU" is an aspiration; with it, it's an auditable configuration.

Controls · 4.4

Guardrails & content safety

In one breath The safety layer that screens what goes into and comes out of the model — blocking or flagging harmful, unsafe, or policy-violating content.

Guardrails are the checks wrapped around inference to keep behavior inside acceptable bounds. They operate at two points:

  • Input filtering — screening prompts for disallowed requests, prompt-injection attempts, or attempts to extract the system prompt.
  • Output filtering — screening the model's response for unsafe content (violence, CSAM, self-harm, certain cyber/bio content) before it reaches the user.

These come in two flavors that reviewers should distinguish:

  • Provider-native safety — built into the model and its API (Claude's safety classifiers and refusals, Google's configurable safety filters). You get these automatically; frontier models sometimes refuse outright, which your app must handle gracefully.
  • Platform guardrails — the policy checks your governance layer adds on top, independent of the model. This is the model-independent-control principle: your policy engine evaluates every call, so safety doesn't depend on any one vendor's built-ins.

The card's "content safety" entry tells you what the model/provider brings; your RAI Trust platform is what guarantees a consistent floor regardless of which model an agent runs on.

Controls · 4.5

PII redaction

In one breath Automatically finding and masking personal details — names, account numbers, health info — before they reach the model or before an answer is stored.

PII is personally identifiable information. PII redaction detects it and replaces it with placeholders ([NAME], [ACCOUNT]) so the sensitive value never travels further than it must.

  • Inbound redaction — strip PII from a prompt before it leaves your boundary for a third-party model, so the provider never sees the raw values.
  • Outbound / storage redaction — mask PII in responses, logs, and audit trails so your own systems don't over-retain it.

It's a layered defense: it complements no-train and residency rather than replacing them. Even under a no-train, in-region deployment, redaction means the highest-sensitivity fields never enter the model at all — the strongest position when a data-subject asks "who saw my data?" the answer is "no system did, it was masked at the door."

Caveat reviewers note: automated detection is never perfect (free-text, typos, and novel formats slip through), so redaction is a risk-reducer, not an absolute guarantee — pair it with residency and no-train, don't rely on it alone.

Controls · 4.6

Rate limits

In one breath Caps on how many requests or tokens you can send per minute. They protect the service — and they can also throttle your agents at the worst moment.

Rate limits are ceilings the provider enforces, usually expressed as RPM (requests per minute), TPM (tokens per minute), and sometimes daily token caps. Two reasons they belong on a governance card:

  • Availability risk. A burst of agent activity — a fan-out of sub-agents, a spike in traffic — can hit the limit and start returning 429 errors. If your agents don't back off and retry gracefully, that's an outage you caused, not the provider.
  • Capacity planning. Limits are tiered by contract; the tier that comfortably served pilot traffic may throttle production. They're a number to negotiate before scale, not discover during an incident.

They also interact with withdrawal risk: a provider can tighten limits or deprioritize your tier, which is a softer form of "withdrawal." Self-hosting removes external rate limits entirely — your only ceiling is your own hardware.

Controls · 4.7

Tool-use gating

In one breath A checkpoint that pauses an agent before it takes a real-world action — sending money, a wire, a shipment — until a policy or a human approves it.

Modern agents don't just chat — they act, by calling tools (transfer funds, place an order, send a message). Tool-use gating is the control that intercepts those actions and requires approval before they execute.

  • Policy gating — your governance engine evaluates each tool call against policy and returns PERMISSIVE / normative / coercive, blocking or escalating high-risk actions automatically.
  • Human-in-the-loop (HITL) — for consequential or ambiguous actions, the agent pauses and a person approves, denies, or asks for more information.

This is where the model card meets the rest of your platform. Gating is deliberately model-independent: whether an agent runs on Llama, Claude, or a sovereign model, the same policy checks and HITL council sit between its intent and any irreversible action. The model can propose; only the governance layer disposes. That's the concrete meaning of "govern the agent, not just trust the model."

Controls · 4.8

Private networking & customer-managed keys (VPC-SC / CMEK)

In one breath Enterprise controls that keep model traffic on a private network and let you hold the encryption keys — so the provider can't decrypt your data without you.

Two related enterprise controls, most visible on cloud-hosted models:

  • Private networking — routing model calls over a private network perimeter instead of the public internet. Google's VPC-SC (VPC Service Controls) is the canonical example: it draws a boundary around your cloud resources so data can't be exfiltrated to services outside it. Equivalents exist as PrivateLink / private endpoints on other clouds.
  • CMEK — customer-managed encryption keys. By default the provider encrypts your data with keys it controls. With CMEK, you own the encryption keys (in your key-management service). Revoke the key and the data becomes unreadable — even to the provider. It turns "trust us with encryption" into "we can't read it without your permission."

These are the controls that get an enterprise security team to yes on a hosted model. They don't change the model's behavior; they harden the pipe and the custody of the data flowing through it — narrowing the gap between a hosted deployment and a self-hosted one.

05
Training data
Training data · 5.1

Training data sources

In one breath What the model learned from before you ever used it. The mix of data shapes its knowledge, its blind spots, and its legal and bias risk.

A model's behavior is downstream of what it was trained on. The card records what's disclosed — which is often only a broad description, because full training-set transparency is rare.

  • Typical mix: large-scale public web text, licensed datasets, code, and (for many models) human-feedback data used to align behavior. Open-weight models publish broad descriptions; frontier models describe theirs only in general terms.
  • Why it's a governance field: the source mix drives bias (over- or under-representation of groups, languages, viewpoints), IP/copyright exposure (was the data licensed?), and capability gaps (a model trained thin on your domain will be weaker there).
  • What you usually can't get: the exact corpus. This opacity is itself a risk to record — you're accepting a model whose full provenance you cannot fully audit, which is one argument for keeping a model-independent policy layer that doesn't depend on trusting any single training pipeline.

For regulated use, note the model's disclosed sources and its documented evaluations rather than assuming — "trained on the internet" is not a compliance answer.

Training data · 5.2

Knowledge cutoff

In one breath The date the model's built-in knowledge stops. Ask it about anything after that and it's guessing unless you give it fresh information.

The knowledge cutoff is the point in time after which the model saw no training data. Everything before it may be "known"; everything after it is not — the model will either say it doesn't know or, worse, confidently invent an answer (a hallucination).

  • It's a staleness control. A model with a 2023 cutoff has no native knowledge of 2024–2026 events, prices, regulations, or product changes. For domains that move fast (sanctions lists, rates, policy), that gap is a correctness and compliance risk.
  • The mitigation is retrieval. You close the gap by feeding current data into the prompt — tool calls, search, retrieval over your own systems — rather than relying on the model's memory. Your agents already do this by pulling live account, shipment, and policy data at request time.
  • Cutoff ≠ release date. A model released in 2026 can still have an earlier knowledge cutoff; check the model's own documentation.

Governance takeaway: never trust a model's built-in memory for anything time-sensitive or authoritative. The cutoff tells you exactly how far you can, and where retrieval must take over.

06
Certifications & compliance
Certifications · 6.1

SOC 2 Type II

In one breath An independent auditor's report that the provider's security controls not only exist but actually worked over a period of months. The baseline "are they serious about security?" badge.

SOC 2 (System and Organization Controls 2) is an audit framework from the AICPA covering five "trust services criteria" — security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy. An independent auditor examines the provider's controls and issues a report.

  • Type I vs Type II — the important distinction. Type I says the controls were designed properly at a single point in time. Type II says the auditor tested that they operated effectively over a period (typically 6–12 months). Type II is the one that counts — it's evidence of practice, not just policy.
  • It's a report, not a pass/fail stamp. You (or your vendor-risk team) can request the actual report under NDA to see scope and any exceptions.
  • What it does and doesn't tell you: it attests to security-program maturity; it does not certify the AI model's safety or accuracy. It's table stakes for enterprise procurement, not a statement about model quality.

On the card, "SOC 2 Type II" on a provider (e.g. Groq, Anthropic, Google Cloud) is the minimum most enterprise security reviews expect before a hosted model can be used with real data.

Certifications · 6.2

ISO/IEC 27001

In one breath The international standard for running a proper information-security program. Certification means an external body verified the provider manages security by the book.

ISO/IEC 27001 is the globally recognized standard for an ISMS — an Information Security Management System. Where SOC 2 produces an auditor's report, ISO 27001 produces a certification against a fixed international standard, issued by an accredited body and re-checked on a cycle.

  • What it covers: a systematic, risk-based approach to securing information — governance, risk assessment, access control, incident response, supplier management, and continual improvement.
  • Related numbers you'll see: 27017 (cloud-specific security) and 27018 (protection of PII in the cloud) are companion standards providers often hold alongside 27001.
  • SOC 2 vs 27001: heavily overlapping. SOC 2 is US-origin and report-based; ISO 27001 is international and certificate-based. Global buyers often want both; European and international procurement lean toward ISO.

For a governance file, ISO 27001 is the internationally portable way to show a provider's security program is externally validated, not self-asserted.

Certifications · 6.3

ISO/IEC 42001

In one breath The first international standard specifically for managing AI responsibly. Think of it as ISO 27001, but for AI governance instead of general security.

ISO/IEC 42001 (published 2023) is the world's first management-system standard for artificial intelligence — an AIMS, AI Management System. It's the newest and most AI-specific badge on the card, and the most directly relevant to what your platform does.

  • What it covers: how an organization governs AI across its lifecycle — risk and impact assessment, data governance, transparency, human oversight, bias management, and continual monitoring of AI systems.
  • Why it's distinct from 27001: 27001 secures information; 42001 governs AI-specific risks (model behavior, automated decisions, AI supply chain) that traditional security standards don't address.
  • Why it's rising fast: it's becoming the reference framework organizations use to demonstrate "responsible AI" to regulators and customers, and it maps closely onto emerging law like the EU AI Act (6.7).

A provider holding ISO 42001 signals they govern their AI, not just their servers — the same posture your RAI Trust platform is built to give you across every model you run.

Certifications · 6.4

HIPAA (and the BAA)

In one breath The US law protecting health information. To put any health data through a model, the provider must sign a specific contract (a BAA) taking on legal responsibility for it.

HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) governs PHI — protected health information — in the US. Providers don't get "HIPAA certified" (there's no such certificate); instead, HIPAA readiness hinges on one document:

  • The BAA — Business Associate Agreement. A contract in which the provider legally commits to HIPAA's safeguards for the PHI you send. Without a signed BAA, you cannot lawfully put PHI through that model, full stop — regardless of how secure it is technically.
  • Scope matters: a provider may offer a BAA only on certain tiers, regions, or with ZDR enabled. "HIPAA (via BAA)" on the card means the option exists — you still have to execute it.
  • It pairs with the data controls: a defensible PHI deployment usually combines a BAA with no-train (4.1), often ZDR (4.2), residency (4.3), and PII handling (4.5).

For a healthcare or pharma workload, this is the gate: no BAA, no PHI, no exceptions.

Certifications · 6.5

GDPR

In one breath Europe's data-protection law. It gives people strong rights over their personal data and applies to anyone handling EU residents' data — with heavy fines for getting it wrong.

GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is the EU's comprehensive personal-data law. Like HIPAA, it's a regulation to comply with, not a certificate to earn — "GDPR" on the card means the provider offers the terms and controls needed to use them compliantly.

  • Core obligations: a lawful basis for processing, data-minimization, purpose limitation, and honoring data-subject rights — access, correction, deletion ("right to erasure"), and portability.
  • What you need from a provider: a Data Processing Agreement (DPA), defined roles (controller vs processor), sub-processor transparency, and lawful handling of any transfer of EU data outside the EU (Standard Contractual Clauses or equivalent).
  • Direct tie to earlier fields: GDPR is why residency (2.2 / 4.3), no-train (4.1), retention limits (4.2), and PII redaction (4.5) are on this card — they're the mechanisms that make an AI deployment defensible under it.
  • Stakes: fines reach up to €20M or 4% of global annual turnover, so this is a board-level risk, not a checkbox.

Practically, GDPR is the lens EU customers use to interrogate every other control here — the certifications and data promises exist largely to answer its questions.

Certifications · 6.6

FedRAMP

In one breath The US government's security stamp for cloud services. If a model provider has it, US federal agencies are allowed to use them — a very high bar to clear.

FedRAMP (Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program) is the standardized security-authorization program a cloud service must clear before US federal agencies can use it. It's one of the most rigorous certifications on the card.

  • Impact levels: authorizations come at Low, Moderate, and High — matching the sensitivity of the data the service is cleared to handle. "FedRAMP High" is the top tier for the most sensitive unclassified federal data.
  • Built on NIST: it's grounded in NIST 800-53 controls and involves continuous monitoring, not a one-time review — an authorization must be maintained.
  • Signal beyond government: even outside the public sector, FedRAMP authorization is read as strong evidence of a mature, heavily audited security posture — useful for regulated commercial buyers too.

On the card, FedRAMP (typically on major cloud hosts like Google Cloud/Vertex) tells you the provider can meet public-sector requirements — relevant if any of your workloads touch government or critical-infrastructure customers.

Certifications · 6.7

EU AI Act

In one breath Europe's new law that regulates AI by how risky its use is. Higher-risk uses carry real legal obligations — and this is the framework your governance work is built to satisfy.

The EU AI Act is the first comprehensive law regulating AI, and unlike the certifications above it targets AI systems specifically. It's risk-tiered — obligations scale with how the AI is used, not what technology it is:

  • Prohibited — a small set of banned uses (e.g. social scoring, certain biometric surveillance).
  • High-risk — uses in areas like credit, employment, and critical infrastructure. These carry the heavy obligations: risk management, data governance, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, and conformity assessment before deployment.
  • Limited-risk — transparency duties (e.g. telling people they're talking to AI, labeling AI-generated content).
  • Minimal-risk — most uses; few or no obligations.
  • GPAI — general-purpose AI models (the foundation models on your cards) carry their own transparency and, for the most capable, systemic-risk obligations on the model makers.

"EU AI Act conformity" on a card (often self-attested for the sovereign option) signals alignment with these obligations. Crucially, the Act's high-risk requirements — logging, human oversight, risk management, traceability — are exactly what your RAI Trust platform provides: audit trails, HITL councils, policy evaluation, and per-agent governance. The model card documents the model's part; your platform delivers the system-level compliance the Act actually demands.
